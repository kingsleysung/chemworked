Calibration curve calculator

Paste your standards (you can copy two columns straight from Excel), add your unknown's readings, and get the line, R², the unknown's concentration and its uncertainty, with every step shown like a lab report.

Signal is whatever you measured: absorbance, peak area, peak-area ratio, etc. Separate the two numbers with a comma, space or tab.

How a calibration curve works

You measure the signal of several standards with known concentrations, fit the best straight line through them, then use that line to turn the unknown's signal into a concentration. For UV-Vis this is Beer's law: absorbance is proportional to concentration, A = εbc.

The least-squares line

With x̄ and ȳ as the averages of the concentrations and signals:

slope m = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ(x − x̄)²
intercept b = ȳ − m·x̄

The uncertainty of your unknown

The scatter of the standards around the line is s_y = √( Σ residual² ÷ (n − 2) ). The standard uncertainty of the unknown's concentration is then:

s_x = (s_y ÷ |m|) · √( 1/k + 1/n + (ȳ_unk − ȳ)² ÷ (m² · Σ(x − x̄)²) )

where k is the number of replicate readings of the unknown and n is the number of standards. Multiply by Student's t (with n − 2 degrees of freedom) for a 95% confidence interval.

Tips for a better result

  • Read the unknown more than once. More replicates (bigger k) shrink the 1/k term, often the biggest one.
  • Aim for the middle of the curve. The uncertainty is smallest when the unknown's signal is near the average signal of the standards. Dilute the unknown if it's above your highest standard.
  • Don't extrapolate. Outside your standards you don't know if the line is still straight.
  • R² isn't everything. A curve can have R² = 0.998 and still be bent. Look at the residuals: they should be small and random, not a smile or frown shape.
